The scams that keep working

Most crypto theft is not a broken cipher. It is a person being polite to the wrong stranger for three weeks.

By the Chainwise desk, 2 November 2026

Every scam below shares three parts: a reason to trust, a reason to hurry, and a claim you cannot check yourself. Remove any one of the three and the scheme stops working. That is why the defence is procedural rather than emotional.

Pig butchering

The long version starts with a wrong number, a dating app match, or a message about a business deal. The conversation is friendly and slow. Weeks later a relative or a mentor mentions an investment platform with steady returns. You open an account, deposit a small amount, and withdraw it successfully. That withdrawal is the hook: it proves the platform pays, which it does, until it does not.

The dashboard shows gains because it is a website, not a market. When you try to withdraw a large balance, a tax, a fee or a verification step appears. Each payment is framed as the last obstacle. The money left your control the first time you sent it.

The signals are consistent. A stranger who is unusually successful and unusually available. A platform you cannot find on independent venues. A return that does not move with the market. An app installed from a download link rather than a store.

Fake support

You post a question in a public forum. Within minutes a helpful account replies with a support link or a direct message. The site looks like the real one. It asks you to connect a wallet, or to install a remote access tool so an agent can see the screen.

No support desk needs your seed phrase, and no legitimate agent needs remote control of your computer. If you opened the chat, close it and go to the exchange’s own help page by typing the address yourself.

Giveaways and impersonation

A live video of a public figure promises to double any coins sent to an address. The video is old footage, the channel is a copy, and the address belongs to the thief. The same script runs during outages, airdrops and major announcements, when attention is high and judgement is low.

Any offer that requires you to send first is a transfer, not a giveaway. There is no exception for verification.

Phishing and approvals

A phishing site can look identical to the one you use, with a domain one character off. It does not need your seed if it can get you to sign. The common request is an approval: permission for a contract to spend a token on your behalf. Some approvals are unlimited, and they stay valid after you close the tab.

Read the request in the wallet. If the site asks for access to a token you did not intend to use, reject it. Review and revoke old approvals from time to time. A wallet that shows a plain “confirm” for an unbounded permission is a wallet that is not helping you.

A signature is a contract. The site that asked for it can disappear. The permission does not.

Token traps and rug pulls

A new token can be built so that buyers cannot sell, so that the creator can mint more, or so that the liquidity can be withdrawn in one transaction. Some projects are honest experiments. Many are a contract designed to take the deposit.

Before buying an unfamiliar token, check who can mint it, whether trading can be paused, how much of the supply the team holds, and whether the liquidity is locked. If those answers are hidden, the price chart is irrelevant.

The recovery scam

After a loss, a new account offers to recover the funds. It asks for a fee, a wallet connection, or a copy of your seed so an “investigator” can trace the transaction. This is the same theft, sold twice. Legitimate recovery is slow, partial and never guaranteed.

SIM swap and account takeover

An attacker persuades a phone carrier to move your number to a new SIM. Password resets by text then arrive at the attacker’s phone. The defence is to remove text messages from the recovery path: use an authenticator app, a hardware security key, and a strong unique password on the email account that controls everything else.

If it already happened

  • Move remaining funds to a wallet you set up on a clean device.
  • Revoke token approvals from a trusted tool, and disconnect the site.
  • Change passwords, starting with email, and remove text based recovery.
  • Contact the exchange or wallet provider from its official page, not from a message.
  • Report to the local police and the national fraud portal. Recovery is unlikely, but the report matters.
  • Do not pay anyone who contacts you first with an offer to help.

The rule that covers most of it

Nobody legitimate ever needs your seed phrase, remote access to your computer, or a payment before a withdrawal. If any of the three appears, stop.
